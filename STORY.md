## The Premise

Qandil is a small lantern spirit. It has no name, no face beyond two dark
eyes, and no weapon. It carries one thing: a lantern, held low in one hand,
and that lantern is the only light left in a place that has gone dark.

The game does not tell this story in cutscenes. There is no dialogue, no
narration track, no text crawl between levels. The story is told by the
worlds themselves — five abandoned places, each one a step further from
warmth and a step closer to something that has been left burning at the top
of a tower.

What the player is doing is simple and does not change: walking forward
through the dark, keeping the flame alive, and reaching the door at the end.

---

## The Character

| Property | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Form | A cloaked spirit, roughly 26 by 34 units |
| Light source | A lantern carried in one hand |
| Movement | Runs, jumps, double jumps |
| Vulnerable to | Spikes, fire, lava, saws, nails, rising stones, falling stones, fragile ground, falling |
| Cannot | Attack, block, or fight in any way |

The spirit has no offensive ability at all. Every hazard in the game must be
avoided or timed, never defeated. This is a deliberate constraint: it keeps
the tension focused entirely on the light and the route.

The lantern in its hand is drawn as a small dark cage with a bright core, so
it reads as an object being carried rather than a glow attached to the
character. The light it casts is what actually lights the level. When the oil
runs low, that light contracts, and the world closes in.

## The Story, World by World

The five worlds are read as a single descent-then-ascent. Each one is a place
that people left, and each one still holds a little of what they left behind.

**Clay House.** Where the journey starts. Low domed houses with warm windows
still faintly lit, straw-flecked mud brick, dust hanging in the air. This is
the gentlest place in the game and it is meant to be — it is where the player
learns that the light is finite and that the ground can be trusted.

**Old Mosque.** Cooler, taller, quieter. Marble slabs with gold inlay,
minarets, shafts of pale light falling from somewhere above. The architecture
gets vertical here and the platforming follows: the first real heights, the
first moments where a missed jump means a fall rather than a stumble.

**The Market.** Colour and noise, or the memory of them. Cloth awnings,
hanging lanterns, banners strung across a magenta dusk. It is the most
crowded world visually and the most crowded with hazards. The market is where
the game stops being forgiving.

**The Ruined Court.** The lights are gone. Broken colonnades, cracked stone,
moss on the edges, violet dust drifting upward instead of down. This world
has the widest gaps and the least mercy. It is the point where the player
either understands the movement system completely or keeps dying.

**Lantern Tower.** The end. Riveted iron, tapering spires, embers rising from
below rather than falling from above. Every mechanic the game has taught is
present here at once, and the final door is the brightest thing in the game.

The tower is the answer to the question the other four worlds ask. Every
place the spirit passed through was dark because the light had been taken
somewhere. It was taken up.

## The Five Worlds in Detail

### World 1 — Clay House

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Levels | 1 – 5 |
| Difficulty band | 0.14 – 0.26 |
| Palette | Warm earth, amber and deep brown |
| Platform material | Rounded mud brick with straw flecks |
| Background | Low domed houses, lit windows, chimneys |
| Atmosphere | Warm dust haze rising from the ground |

The teaching world — but "teaching" no longer means "empty". Every hazard
family is present from level 1, in its gentlest form: one slow saw over open
ground, one short nail row, one wide rising stone with a long fuse. The player
meets each mechanic while there is still room to recover from getting it wrong.

The controls tutorial runs here, once, on level 1, and advances the moment the
player actually performs each action rather than on a timer. The hazard cards
are separate: they fire wherever the player first *sees* the thing, which may
well be here and may well be in world 3.

### World 2 — Old Mosque

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Levels | 6 – 10 |
| Difficulty band | 0.31 – 0.42 |
| Palette | Cool teal, jade and near-black |
| Platform material | Polished marble with a gold inlay line |
| Background | Minarets, arcades of pointed arches |
| Atmosphere | Pale shafts of light falling from above |

Verticality arrives. Floating islands and staircases become common, and
ceilings start appearing — low slabs that punish careless jumping. Fire becomes
a regular hazard rather than an occasional one, and the saw count climbs from
one to two.

The contrast with World 1 is deliberate: the same mechanics, a colder light,
and a much longer drop when you miss.

### World 3 — The Market

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Levels | 11 – 15 |
| Difficulty band | 0.47 – 0.60 |
| Palette | Magenta, rose and deep plum |
| Platform material | Wooden planks with cloth trim hanging off the edge |
| Background | Market awnings on poles, hanging lanterns |
| Atmosphere | Colourful cloth banners strung across the sky |

The midpoint, and the hardest world relative to what came before it. Gaps widen
past the comfortable single-jump range and start demanding the second jump.
Ceiling nails arrive here, which is the first hazard that forbids jumping
outright — the player has to walk under it, not over it.

This is where the game expects the player to stop treating the double jump as
a rescue and start treating it as part of the route.

### World 4 — The Ruined Court

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Levels | 16 – 20 |
| Difficulty band | 0.66 – 0.80 |
| Palette | Violet, slate and cold indigo |
| Platform material | Cracked stone blocks with moss on the top edge |
| Background | Shattered colonnades of uneven height, rubble |
| Atmosphere | Violet dust drifting upward |

Everything is in play here. Levels are at their widest and the quotas are at
their highest: up to eight spike runs, five fires, five saws, five nail rows,
five rising stones and three falling stones in a single level.

The ruined court is the world most likely to end a run. It is also the world
where a player who has learned the movement system starts moving through it
quickly, because the gaps are large but readable.

### World 5 — Lantern Tower

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Levels | 21 – 25 |
| Difficulty band | 0.85 – 1.00 |
| Palette | Ember orange, rust and black |
| Platform material | Riveted iron with a glowing ember line |
| Background | Tapering spires with iron rings and lit windows |
| Atmosphere | Embers rising from below |

Expert tier. Every segment type is in the pool, every hazard category is at
quota, and the levels are the longest in the game at 11,600 units. Nothing new
is introduced here — the tower is a test of everything already taught, run at
full density.

What changes at this tier is not the rules but the numbers: blades at their
fastest, fuses at their shortest, falling stones at their deepest drop, and the
gauntlet segment — saw, nails, riser, crusher, saw in one unbroken run —
appearing regularly for the first time.

The final level, **The Last Dawn**, ends the same way every level does: at a
glowing door. The difference is that there is no next level, and the results
screen says so.

## The Twenty-Five Levels

Difficulty is expressed as a single number from 0.14 to 1.00, computed as
`min(1.00, 0.14 + (index/24)^1.30 * 0.86)`. It rises across the set and also
rises within each level, so the second half of any level is harder than its
first half.

| # | World | Level | `baseDiff` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Clay House | First Spark | 0.14 |
| 2 | Clay House | A Separate Step | 0.16 |
| 3 | Clay House | Stair of Shadows | 0.19 |
| 4 | Clay House | Hanging Lamp | 0.21 |
| 5 | Clay House | Last Light | 0.26 |
| 6 | Old Mosque | The Arch | 0.31 |
| 7 | Old Mosque | Abandoned Niche | 0.34 |
| 8 | Old Mosque | Well of Light | 0.36 |
| 9 | Old Mosque | Shard of Glass | 0.39 |
| 10 | Old Mosque | Dawn Hour | 0.42 |
| 11 | The Market | Market Lanterns | 0.47 |
| 12 | The Market | Silk Rope | 0.50 |
| 13 | The Market | Long Night | 0.53 |
| 14 | The Market | Shadow Dance | 0.57 |
| 15 | The Market | Daybreak | 0.60 |
| 16 | The Ruined Court | Gate of Sand | 0.66 |
| 17 | The Ruined Court | Inverted Lamps | 0.69 |
| 18 | The Ruined Court | Corridor of Thorns | 0.73 |
| 19 | The Ruined Court | Freefall | 0.76 |
| 20 | The Ruined Court | Eye of the Storm | 0.80 |
| 21 | Lantern Tower | The First Stair | 0.85 |
| 22 | Lantern Tower | Twin Lanterns | 0.88 |
| 23 | Lantern Tower | Dark Summit | 0.91 |
| 24 | Lantern Tower | Heart of the Lantern | 0.95 |
| 25 | Lantern Tower | The Last Dawn | 1.00 |

Level width scales with the index, from 8,400 units at level 1 to 11,600 at
level 25. The exponent is what makes the last five levels steep: level 20 to
25 covers a wider difficulty span than levels 1 to 15.

### The Thirteen Segment Types

Every level is assembled from these, mixed by the segment picker.

| Segment | What it tests | Appears at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `run` | Ground hazards, reading a hazard at speed | 0.00 |
| `gap` | Jump distance, the core skill | 0.00 |
| `stairs` | Vertical movement, timing on ascent and descent | 0.02 |
| `island` | Precision on small platforms over a void | 0.05 |
| `nails` | Restraint — a ceiling row forbids jumping | 0.08 |
| `saws` | Reading a fixed patrol and crossing between passes | 0.10 |
| `corridor` | Low ceilings that punish careless jumping | 0.12 |
| `mover` | Patience and prediction | 0.15 |
| `lava` | Chaining jumps across stepping stones | 0.18 |
| `crumble` | Forward momentum — stopping is fatal | 0.20 |
| `rise` | Committing to a surface that will not wait | 0.22 |
| `crushers` | Crossing under a stone that is about to fall | 0.25 |
| `gauntlet` | Everything at once, in a readable order | 0.30 |

**Every family is available from level 1.** The `Appears at` column is not a
gate the player has to reach — it is the difficulty at which the template
starts showing up in the mix, and every value is low enough that level 1
already qualifies for all of them. What scales is intensity: the segment
functions read `diff` and change their own count, speed, travel and fuse.

The picker never repeats the same segment twice in a row, and gives a
temporary boost to a segment that has just unlocked so the player actually
meets the new mechanic instead of missing it in the shuffle. The weights on
`run` and `gap` *decay* with difficulty (1.60 → 0.50), so the back half of the
game is genuinely dense rather than mostly open floor.

### Hazard Quotas

After a level is assembled, a top-up pass guarantees a minimum count of each
hazard family based on the level's difficulty:

| Hazard | Quota |
| --- | --- |
| Spikes | 3 to 8 |
| Fire | 1 to 6 |
| Moving platforms | 1 to 3 |
| Lava pools | 1 to 3 |
| Nails | 1 to 6 |
| Saws | 1 to 5 |
| Rising stones | 1 to 6 |
| Falling stones | 1 to 4 |

**Every family has a floor of at least 1 at every difficulty.** No level can
be missing a mechanic. This exists because the segment picker is
probabilistic — without the top-up pass, a level could come out with no fires
at all, which would flatten the curve for that level and, worse, let a player
reach world 3 without ever having met a saw.

Two guards keep the top-up pass from making a level look random:

- **Attempts.** Each missing hazard gets up to four placement attempts with a
  fresh seed, across four sweeps at a 90-unit step. A single failed sweep no
  longer abandons the whole family.
- **Spacing.** Before anything is placed, `_nearHazard()` checks for an
  existing hazard within a 110-unit window and refuses the placement. The
  density in a level comes from the segment templates, which are authored;
  the top-up pass is only allowed to fill genuine gaps.

## Difficulty Curve

The curve is built from four independent layers that stack.

**Across the set.** Base difficulty rises on a convex curve,
`0.14 + (index/24)^1.30 * 0.86`, from 0.14 at level 1 to 1.00 at level 25.
This controls which segment types are in the pool and how many hazards are
guaranteed.

The curve is deliberately not linear. A linear ramp spreads the same 0.80 of
difficulty evenly across 24 steps, so no single level ever feels like a step
up. The exponent `1.30` front-loads the gentle part and back-loads the steep
part: levels 1–10 cover 0.14 → 0.42, while levels 20–25 cover 0.80 → 1.00.
The endgame is a different game from the opening, and the player can feel the
moment it changes.

**Within a level.** Difficulty is modulated by progress through the level:
`baseDiff + (progress − 0.5) × 0.22`. So the opening of a level is slightly
easier than its band and the final stretch is noticeably harder. The hardest
moment in any level is always the last third.

**Through segment intensity.** Every template is available from the start;
what changes is how hard each one is when it appears:

```
segment        level 1                          level 25
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
saws           1 blade, slow, open floor        4 blades, fast, broken floor
nails          1 short floor row                 6 rows + ceiling slabs
rise           2 wide stones, long fuse          5 narrow stones, tight fuse
crushers       1 stone, slow, short drop         4 stones, fast, deep drop
crumble        3 platforms, ~0.95s fuse          chain of 5, ~0.40s fuse
```

Each new template still gets a weighted boost for a short window after it
enters the pool, so the player meets it several times in quick succession and
learns it properly rather than encountering it once and forgetting it.

**Through hazard density.** The quotas grow with difficulty, so a late level
is not just longer — it has more things in it per unit of distance.

The result is a curve that starts as a walk and ends as a test of everything
the game has taught, without a single spike in difficulty that feels unfair.
The important property is that nothing is ever introduced and then abandoned:
`mover` and `lava` in particular carry extra weight in the picker so they
cannot vanish from late levels.

## How to Play

### The Loop

1. Enter a level with five hearts and a full tank of oil.
2. Move right. Collect gems. Collect oil flasks.
3. Touch checkpoints to save your respawn position.
4. Reach the glowing door at the end.

### Controls

| Action | Keyboard | Touch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Move left | `Left Arrow` / `A` | Left pad |
| Move right | `Right Arrow` / `D` | Right pad |
| Jump | `Space` / `Up Arrow` / `W` | Jump button |
| Pause | `Escape` / `P` | Pause icon |

### What Kills You

Nine hazard families, each with one rule. Every one appears from level 1.

| Hazard | The rule |
| --- | --- |
| Spikes | Static, lethal on contact. Jump it. |
| Fire | Lights and dies on a steady rhythm. Read the beat, cross while it is out. |
| Lava | Never goes out. Cross on the stones or not at all. |
| Saws | A spinning blade on a fixed patrol. Watch the far end of its travel. |
| Nails | Dense short rows, floor or ceiling. The ceiling kind forbids jumping. |
| Rising stones | Erupt as you approach, crumble after a fuse. Touch and go. |
| Falling stones | Drop when you pass under. The red rail shows where. |
| Fragile platforms | Crumble after a variable delay. The cracks glow first. |
| Moving platforms | Carry you over voids. Their motion is regular and learnable. |

**Falling** below the bottom of the view costs a heart.

**The timing rule.** Fragile platforms and rising stones each carry their own
fuse and a respawn flag, so half of them come back and half never do. A player
who memorises one chain will die on the next; a player who understands "keep
moving" will cross any of them.

**The fairness floor.** No fuse is shorter than 0.30 s. That is not a tuning
choice, it is a promise: below that threshold the player cannot physically
react, and the death stops being their fault.

### Learning the Hazards

The controls are taught in six steps on the first level. The hazards are not
taught in a sequence at all — each one introduces itself the first time the
player actually sees it, wherever that happens to be. A card appears in the
corner, says what the thing is and how to beat it, and then leaves. It does
not pause the game, and it never comes back.

The idea is that a lesson lands hardest at the moment it is needed. A player
reading about saws while standing in front of a saw will remember it. The
same player reading about saws on level 1, with no saw in sight, will not.

### The Two Things That Run Out

**Hearts.** Five per level. Losing one respawns you at the last checkpoint you
touched, with 1.4 seconds of invulnerability so you cannot chain-die
instantly. Lose all five and the level ends — you choose between retrying it
from the start and returning to the menu.

**Oil.** Drains at 1.6 units per second from a maximum of 100, so a full tank
lasts about 62 seconds. Each flask restores 38 units. As oil drops, the light
radius around you contracts. Running out does not kill you, but it makes the
level unreadable, which amounts to the same thing.

### Practical Advice

- **Do not hoard the double jump.** It refills the instant you touch ground.
  Use it to extend a jump you have already committed to.
- **Watch the light, not the meter.** The oil bar tells you the number, but
  the shrinking radius tells you whether you can still see the next platform.
- **Read a hazard before you reach it.** Fire lights its own surroundings
  through the darkness layer, so a pulsing glow ahead means time your approach
  rather than run at it.
- **Never stop on a fragile platform or a rising stone.** A crumble chain is a
  rhythm, not a set of individual jumps.
- **Read a saw's far end, not its centre.** The blade is always somewhere in
  the middle of its travel. What you need to know is where it will be when you
  arrive.
- **A ceiling nail row means walk, not jump.** The instinct to hop is exactly
  what kills you there.
- **A falling stone's red rail is the whole answer.** It shows the landing
  spot before the stone moves. Cross when the stone is raised; never step into
  the rail while it is coming down.
- **Checkpoints are lanterns on poles, not flags.** They sit 30 units above
  solid ground and are never placed on anything that can break.
- **The camera leads you.** It keeps the character at about 42% of the screen
  width, so you always see further ahead than behind. Trust what you can see
  on the right.

## Scoring and Mastery

| Stars | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Reach the door |
| 2 | Reach the door with every gem in the level |
| 3 | Reach the door with every gem, within par time |

Par time is not a flat multiplier. It is computed per level as:

```
par = (width / 320) × (1.95 + difficulty × 0.85)
      + spikes   × 1.6
      + fires    × 2.0
      + lava     × 2.0
      + movers   × 3.0
      + nails    × 1.4
      + saws     × 2.6
      + risers   × 2.2
      + crushers × 3.0
```

The first term is travel time scaled by difficulty. The second group adds a
per-hazard cost, weighted by how much each one actually slows a competent
player down: a saw or a crusher costs more than a spike because it forces a
wait, not just a jump. This is why a three-star run on level 25 is demanding
but genuinely reachable, rather than mathematically impossible as it would be
under a flat multiplier.

Best time is recorded per level. A new best overwrites the old one, and a
better star count is never downgraded by a worse run.

Level *n* unlocks when level *n − 1* has at least one star. Finishing a level
is therefore the only requirement for progression; stars are for players who
want the whole thing.

## How Long It Takes

These figures come from the level dimensions and the movement constants.

### Per Level

| Measure | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Level width | 8,400 to 11,600 world units |
| Run speed | 320 units/s |
| Effective speed | roughly 65% of maximum, accounting for jumps, corrections and pauses |
| Pure traversal time | 40 to 56 seconds |
| Realistic first clear | 2 to 5 minutes |
| Realistic clean run | 1 to 2 minutes |

### Full Playthrough

| Playstyle | Estimated time |
| --- | --- |
| Deathless run, all 25 levels, no retries | about 20 minutes |
| First playthrough, learning each level | 1.5 to 2.5 hours |
| All 25 levels at three stars | 3 to 5 hours |
| Completionist, including best-time chasing | open-ended |

The twenty-minute figure is the design target for a perfect run: 25 levels
averaging roughly 10,000 units each, at an effective 208 units/s, works out to
around 1,200 seconds of pure movement. Everything above that is the cost of
learning.

### Where the Time Goes

- **Worlds 1 and 2** are fast. A confident player clears each level on the
  first or second attempt.
- **World 3** is where retries start. Moving platforms and lava require
  waiting for a window, which adds real time even to a clean run.
- **World 4** is the time sink on a first playthrough. Wide levels, dense
  hazards, and a checkpoint spacing of 900 units means a death in the middle
  costs a meaningful chunk of progress.
- **World 5** is fast for a player who has mastered the movement and slow for
  one who has not. There is no new information here, only density.

## Design Philosophy

A few principles shaped everything above.

**The light is the game.** The oil meter is not a timer bolted onto a
platformer. It is the thing that decides how much of the level you can see,
and therefore how much of the level you can plan. Every other system is
subordinate to it.

**Nothing is placed blind.** Every platform, hazard, gem, flask and checkpoint
in every level passes a validation call before it is committed. A hazard must
rest on real ground, leave headroom above it, and have nothing suspended over
its footprint. A checkpoint must sit on solid ground that cannot crumble, be
clear of hazards on both sides, and be far enough from the previous one to
matter. The builder refuses bad placements rather than placing them and hoping.

**Determinism over randomness.** Levels are generated from a seeded PRNG, so
they are the same for everyone, every run. This means a level can be learned,
a route can be optimised, and a three-star time can be chased. Procedural does
not have to mean unpredictable.

**Hazards must be legible.** Every hazard is drawn so it reads as danger
before the player reaches it, and every one punches its own hole in the
darkness layer so the lantern reveals it before it can kill you:

- **Fire** lights its own surroundings and keeps its embers drifting even
  while the flame is out, so the pit never disappears.
- **Fragile platforms** always show hairline cracks; they widen and glow
  orange during the countdown.
- **Rising stones** throw a dust plume while climbing, then use the same
  crack-and-glow language as a fragile platform.
- **Falling stones** carry a dashed red rail showing exactly where they will
  land, before they ever move.
- **Saws** have twelve real teeth, a hot rim and a hazard glow.
- **Nails** are thinner and brighter than spikes, with a visible base rail, so
  the two are never confused.
- **Moving platforms** have chains above them, direction chevrons on the deck,
  and a lit underside.

Nothing in the game is dangerous without looking dangerous first. A hazard the
player cannot see is not a hazard, it is a trap.

**The character cannot fight.** No combat, no power-ups, no upgrades. The
spirit only has movement and light, and the whole game is built to make those
two things enough.
